TL;DR

One UX bug eclipses every other friction signal on the site.

The "Sign Up" / "Join CarGo" sign-up card on the homepage is generating 60,583 dead clicks on the parent card and 52,838 dead clicks on the "Sign Up" word itself this week alone. Two orders of magnitude larger than the next signal. Users are clicking text styled like an action — and nothing is happening.

Page load time regressed from 3.7s to 18.2s week-over-week. That's the second headline. Could be data quality (small sample on slow pages) or a real performance regression worth a separate engineering investigation.

Friction-clicker counts dropped, but so did total users. Per-user friction rates held roughly steady. Net: no improvement, less traffic.

Recommendations

Three things to fix this week

Ranked by user impact and ease of resolution. Each links to the underlying Fullstory metric for verification.

P1

Make the "Sign Up" / "Join CarGo" surface a real button

Combined volume across both row variants: 113,421 dead clicks this week — 99.8% of all dead clicks on the site. Users see an action-styled card and click; nothing happens. Either wire the entire card to onClick → /signup, or visually de-emphasize it so users stop reading it as a CTA.

P1

Investigate the +388% page-load regression

TW avg 18.2s vs LW 3.7s, same metric definition both weeks. Likely candidates: a slow new third-party tag, a CDN issue, or a sampling artifact from a small number of pathological sessions. Slice by URL_PATH and by browser to isolate.

P2

Fix the "Add to cart" JS error

122 error clicks this week on elements labeled "Add to cart" — almost certainly on cargomerch.myshopify.com or catformalwear.myshopify.com. A single bug fix could clear the entire error-click signal on this site.

P3

Audit the "Available" status badge for clickability

Top rage-click target both weeks (1,418 TW, 2,971 LW). Users are clicking a status word on a car listing expecting it to start the booking flow. Either make it a real CTA or remove it from the click target hierarchy. The price-amount pattern ("$1200", "$180", etc.) directly underneath shows the same behavior — users click prices expecting action.
